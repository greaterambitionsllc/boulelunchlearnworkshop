Meeting Facilitation:
Before & After Boule

Most planning teams spend months in meetings. Very few of those meetings are actually well-run. A meeting that does not produce a decision, a clear owner, or a next action is not a meeting — it is a conversation with a calendar invite. The facilitation standards that move work forward do not change based on timing. But what each meeting needs to produce is completely different depending on where you are in the cycle.

Pre-Boule Meetings

Phase Two
Post-Boule Meetings
Process what happened, capture lessons, and close the cycle with the team
Pre-Boule
Running the Meetings That Build the Plan
Planning through final lock
01
Set the Objective Before You Set the Agenda

Most meeting agendas are topic lists. What they are missing is the answer to one question: what does this meeting need to produce? A decision. A confirmed handoff. A locked plan. If you cannot name the output before the meeting starts, you are not ready to call the meeting. An agenda without an objective is an invitation to talk in circles. Every pre-Boule planning meeting should have a stated output in the invite — not just a list of topics, but the specific thing that needs to exist when the meeting ends that did not exist when it started.

Facilitator Practice Open every meeting with one sentence: "When we leave today, we need to have decided / confirmed / completed ___." Say it out loud. Write it on a shared screen. It anchors the room and gives everyone a shared definition of a productive hour.
02
Clarity Is Not Optional — It Is the Output

If people are leaving a meeting still guessing about what was decided, who owns it, or what happens next — the meeting failed. It does not matter how much was discussed. Discussion is not a deliverable. Clarity is. When you are facilitating, your job is not to run through the agenda. Your job is to make sure nothing leaves the room ambiguous. Assumptions do not scale. Clarity does. And clarity is a discipline — it does not happen naturally in group settings. Someone has to manufacture it on purpose, every single meeting.

Facilitator Practice End every agenda item with a thirty-second close: "What was decided? Who owns it? What is the deadline?" Do not move to the next item until those three questions have a spoken answer. If anyone gives a different answer, you have not reached clarity — you have reached the illusion of it.
03
Communication Is Not About Sending. It Is About Landing.

You can send ten messages and still not be understood. You can hold five meetings and still not have alignment. The measure of communication is not how much was transmitted — it is whether it landed the way it needed to. The loudest voice in the room is often not the one carrying the most critical information. The quietest person at the table may be sitting on the risk nobody has named yet. A skilled facilitator creates the conditions for the right information to surface — not just the most comfortable version of it. That means actively drawing out the people who are not speaking, and never confusing room silence with room agreement.

Facilitator Practice Before closing any major decision, do a one-round check: ask each person to confirm their understanding in one sentence. When people say it back in their own words, gaps in alignment surface while there is still time to close them.
04
The Project Manager Asking Questions Is Not Being Difficult

One of the most misunderstood behaviors in planning meetings is the PM who keeps asking questions after the group thinks the conversation is done. That is not obstruction. That is the job. A project manager's mind is trained to find the cracks before execution exposes them. When a PM asks again, follows up again, or requests more detail, it is because unanswered questions in planning become real problems in execution. The facilitator's role is to protect that process — not rush past it because the room wants to move on. If a question is being asked, it deserves an answer. If the answer does not exist yet, that is the most important thing the meeting produced.

Facilitator Practice Keep a visible parking lot — a running list of questions raised that do not yet have answers. Every item gets an owner and a deadline. Parking lot items are tracked, not dismissed. An unanswered question that gets followed up is infinitely better than one that gets ignored and resurfaces on the conference floor.
05
Close Every Meeting With a Written Record — Not a Memory

Verbal agreements in pre-Boule meetings have a short shelf life. By the next morning, two people will remember the same decision differently. By next week, three people will have acted on three different versions of what was agreed. Meeting notes are not administrative overhead — they are the mechanism that keeps the team moving in the same direction. Every pre-Boule planning meeting ends with a written summary: decisions made, owners assigned, deadlines confirmed. Sent within 24 hours. This is different from the execution day issues log, which captures problems in real time on the conference floor. Planning notes capture alignment before execution begins.

Facilitator Practice Designate a dedicated note-taker — someone whose only job is to capture decisions, owners, and next steps in real time. Do not facilitate and document simultaneously. The output suffers when one person carries both roles. Notes go out the same day. No reply confirmation means the loop is still open.

Execution Readiness

Grounding the Team
Before Game Day

You have done the planning. The contracts are signed. The run-of-show is built. The schedule is set. But there is a space between planning complete and execution ready — and too many teams skip it. These four readiness checks are what close that gap. What it means to truly be ready. What the team needs to check internally before Boule begins.

🎯 Readiness Check 01
Know Your Role. Own Your Lane.

One of the most common sources of confusion during execution is a team member who was assigned a role in planning but never truly internalized it. They know the task list. They do not know the weight of what they are carrying. Before Boule begins, every person on the planning team should be able to answer three questions without hesitation:
1. What do I own?
2. What can I decide on my own?
3. Who do I go to when something is above my authority?
If the answer to any of those is unclear, that gap closes now — not on the conference floor.

Before Boule Opens Each lead reads their role scope aloud in the final team meeting. Hearing it spoken is different from having read it once. It activates ownership in a way that silent review does not.
🧱 Readiness Check 02
Decide the Hard Things Now.
Not in the Moment.
Lock What Needs to Be Locked.

There is a specific kind of stress that comes from entering execution with open items — tasks still in progress, confirmations still pending, decisions still circling. That stress is not inevitable. It is the product of things that should have been closed and were not. Decisions like: what happens if a speaker cancels the morning of? What is the protocol if registration lines get out of control? Who makes the call if a session runs long and the next room is not ready? These are not hypotheticals. They are the normal texture of live events. The goal of the final stretch before Boule is not to keep working at the same pace. It is to reach a locked state — where every critical item has been completed, confirmed, and handed off. A team that arrives at locked is a team that can execute freely and confidently. A team that arrives with a list is a team that is still planning during execution.

Before Boule Opens Two weeks out, run a 20-minute "what if" conversation as a team. Audit every task in progress. Name the five scenarios that worry the room most. Decide the answer to each one right now. Write it down. Anything still undecided at 72 hours before go-time is an escalation item — not a to-do.
💡 Readiness Check 03
Get Clear on What Success Looks Like — For You.

The team has been focused on what success looks like for the attendees. That matters. But each person on the planning team also needs a personal definition of what a successful Boule looks like for them. Not in terms of tasks completed — in terms of how they want to carry themselves. Calm under pressure. Helpful to their team. Present for the moments they worked so hard to create. When that picture is clear before execution starts, it functions as a compass. When something goes sideways — and something will — that personal standard is what the team member returns to instead of reacting from stress.

Before Boule Opens Take two minutes in the final team meeting. Ask everyone to write down one sentence: "At the end of Boule, I will feel successful if I _____." Not a task. A way of being. Read them aloud if the team is willing.
🤲 Readiness Check 04
Acknowledge What the Team
Has Already Done.

Teams walk into execution carrying the weight of everything that came before it — the late meetings, the vendor negotiations, the last-minute changes, the moments nobody outside this room saw. That weight is real. And it needs to be named before the team steps onto the conference floor. Not as a complaint. As a recognition. This team built something. The planning cycle is a legitimate accomplishment before a single attendee arrives. When that truth is acknowledged out loud — by the team, for the team — it shifts the energy from exhausted to proud. And proud teams execute differently than depleted ones.

Before Boule Opens Open your last pre-Boule team meeting with a round of recognition. Not generic praise — name what each person contributed that mattered. It is not wasted time. It is fuel.

Take a Moment. Answer These Honestly.

These are not rhetorical. Use them in your final team meeting before Boule opens — individually first, then discuss as a group. What surfaces is the work that still needs to happen.

Question 01

"If Boule started tomorrow, what is the one thing keeping me up at night — and does the team know about it?"

Question 02

"Do I know what I am fully responsible for — and what I am not responsible for — on execution day?"

Question 03

"Am I arriving to Boule with a locked plan — or am I arriving with a list of things I hope work out?"

Question 04

"Who on this team needs more support right now — and have they said anything about it?"

Planning Philosophy
Planning Best Practices New

What Disciplined Planning
Actually Looks Like

Planning is not the meeting you hold before the real work starts. Planning is the real work. What follows is the difference between a plan that holds under pressure and one that quietly breaks the moment execution begins.

🔍 Practice 01
Treat Small Details Like the Infrastructure They Are

There is no such thing as a minor detail in large-scale planning — only visible work and invisible work. The follow-up email nobody thought was necessary. The confirmation that felt redundant. The ClickUp notification that seemed like overkill. In planning, these feel like extra. In execution, they are the difference between flow and friction. Teams that dismiss small tasks as administrative noise are the same teams that spend execution day scrambling to reconstruct information they should have had locked down weeks earlier. Every small detail that gets tracked, confirmed, and closed in planning is one less fire to manage in real time.

What This Looks Like in Practice Do not close a planning task until the confirmation exists in writing. Not a verbal agreement. Not a "I think they said yes." A written confirmation with a name and a date. That extra five minutes in planning saves forty minutes of damage control on execution day.
📐 Practice 02
Solidify the Plan Before You Enter the Trenches

Not everything will be perfect. Every variable will not be controlled. But you can get close — and getting close matters. Because once execution is moving in real time, that is not the moment to still be building what should have already been outlined. That is not the moment to be guessing. That is not the moment to be making decisions that should have already been made weeks ago. Planning will never remove every problem. But it eliminates the preventable ones. The standard is not a perfect plan — it is a complete plan. A plan where the critical decisions are already made, the critical questions are already answered, and the team is not walking into execution still in discovery mode.

What This Looks Like in Practice At four weeks out, conduct a plan completeness audit. Not a status update — a structural check. Is every workstream owned? Is every contingency documented? Is every vendor confirmed in writing? Any gap at four weeks is a manageable risk. The same gap at four days is a crisis.
⚠️ Practice 03
Execution Will Always Expose the Truth About the Planning

Not the polished version. Not the hopeful version. The real version. The schedule will tell the truth. The transitions will tell the truth. The response time will tell the truth. Execution has a way of revealing whether the foundation was solid or shaky all along — and it reveals it publicly, in front of attendees, vendors, and leadership. Every gap left open in planning will surface. The only question is whether it surfaces during a planning meeting — where it costs a conversation — or during execution — where it costs composure, time, and trust.

What This Looks Like in Practice Run a truth audit before you lock the plan. Ask the team: if execution started tomorrow, what would break first? Name it specifically. Then close that gap before it closes itself on the conference floor.
🧩 Practice 04
The Visible Work Is Only Half the Plan

Every conference has a public-facing plan — the schedule, the agenda, the speaker lineup, the program. That is the visible work. What holds it together is the invisible work: the task nobody sees that makes the visible task possible. The AV check that happens two hours before the session. The volunteer briefing that happens the morning of. The registration backup process built in case the primary system goes down. Teams that plan only the visible work arrive to execution realizing the invisible work was never assigned, never confirmed, and never owned. That invisible layer is where most live events break — not in the scheduled moments, but in everything that was supposed to support them.

What This Looks Like in Practice For every major scheduled moment, list the three to five invisible tasks that make it possible. Assign each one. Confirm each one. A scheduled session without a confirmed AV owner and a tested backup is a risk wearing a program agenda.
⚡ Practice 05
Energy Is a Planning Variable — Not a Personal Problem

Fatigue shows up in missed details. Burnout shows up in delayed decisions. These are not character flaws — they are predictable outputs of a planning process that did not account for human capacity. The team managing Boule is not a machine. They have a finite amount of decision-making bandwidth per day. A planning schedule that demands sustained high-output over too many consecutive weeks will degrade the quality of the decisions being made — not dramatically, but quietly. The small things start getting skipped. The follow-up does not go out. The confirmation does not get requested. You manage the work. You also manage the people doing the work.

What This Looks Like in Practice In your final eight weeks of planning, identify the two or three highest cognitive-demand weeks on the schedule. Protect the team's bandwidth in those windows. Do not schedule unrelated decisions or heavy administrative work during peak planning periods. Energy is a resource. Plan for it the same way you plan for budget.
👁 Practice 06
People Will Not See the Work. But They Will Feel the Results.

The attendees will never know what it took. They will not know about the vendor who confirmed three days late, the AV setup that ran two hours over, or the last-minute schedule rebuild. What they will know is how it felt to be there. Whether the transitions were smooth. Whether the space felt prepared. Whether the team they encountered seemed in control or in chaos. That gap — between what was carried and what was experienced — is the whole discipline of conference planning. The work that nobody sees is the work that everybody feels. That is the standard.

What This Looks Like in Practice Before finalizing the plan, walk the attendee experience from arrival to departure. Not the planner's view — the attendee's view. What do they see first? What is the energy at registration? Every friction point in that walk is a planning gap. Find it now.

The Heart of This Workshop

The Sanity Behind
Seamless Execution

Seamless does not happen by accident. It happens because someone — or a whole team of someones — decided to do the disciplined, unglamorous, invisible work required to make it look easy. That is you. And that work costs something. This section is about understanding what makes execution feel manageable, protecting the people doing it, and building the composure to handle it when it does not go as planned.

Pillar 1 of 3

The Architecture of
Seamless Execution

Every moment that "just flows" at Boule was engineered. Here is how that engineering actually works — and what breaks it.

🗺
Map Every Moment, Not Just the Major Ones

Seamless execution is built in the transitions — the thirty-minute window between the end of one session and the start of the next, the moment someone reaches the registration desk and nobody is there, the silence before the AV picks up. Those micro-moments are where execution either holds or breaks. Milestone planning gets the team to the event. Micro-moment planning is what the attendee actually experiences. The run-of-show is the map. If a moment is not on the run-of-show, no one owns it. And if no one owns it, it will surface on the floor without a plan behind it.

What to do Walk the run-of-show as a team and identify every gap between named moments. For each one: who owns it, what the cue is, and what smooth looks like. Assign a human being — not a role, a name — to every critical moment on the schedule. What is not assigned is not managed. Script every transition, not just the events.
🔂
Stop Performing Calm. Build the Conditions for It.

There is a difference between a team that looks calm during execution and a team that is actually calm. The first is performance. The second is the result of preparation. Teams that are genuinely steady on the floor are steady because they walked in having already made the hard calls — the contingency decisions, the escalation protocols, the problem-first norm. They are not improvising. They are executing a response they already have. That composure cannot be willed into existence on execution day. It has to be built before the team ever steps on the floor.

What to do In the final team meeting, say out loud: during execution, we solve the problem first and debrief after. Teams that agree on this before the conference do not waste time on it during it.
📡
Build a Communication Command Structure for Execution Day

During execution, communication cannot flow the same way it did in planning. Everyone cannot be in every conversation. Decisions need to be made in seconds, not meetings. The team needs a clear chain — who communicates what to whom, who has decision authority in each area, and what gets escalated versus what gets handled on the floor. Without that structure, information bottlenecks at the top and the people closest to the problem are left guessing.

What to do Create a one-page execution day communication map. Name the lead for each workstream, the decisions they can make independently, and the single escalation contact for anything above their authority. Print it. Everyone carries it.

"Execution does not break because of the big things. It breaks because of the small things nobody tracked, nobody owned, and nobody prepared for. Close those gaps in planning — and your team gets to breathe during execution."
~ Dr. Jaye

Seamless Execution Principle
Pillar 2 of 3

Protecting the Team
During Execution

The planning team is the infrastructure of Boule. If that infrastructure is not protected, the event does not hold. These are the operational practices that keep the team functional when the pressure is at its highest.

🔋
Energy is a Non-Renewable Resource During Event Week

The team will arrive at conference week with a finite amount of energy. How that energy is deployed determines how much is left by the time the highest-demand moments arrive. Every task that should have been completed in planning but was not will drain from that reserve. Every decision that should have been pre-made but was not will cost more energy under pressure than it would have cost in a planning meeting. Protect the reserve by finishing the work early.

What to do Audit task completion two weeks before the event. Any task still in progress at that point is a risk. Push hard to close everything before conference week begins so the team arrives with energy instead of a list.
🔄
Rotate. Rest. Recover. Repeat.

No single team member should hold a high-demand position for the entire duration of the conference. This is not a preference — it is a performance requirement. Fatigue degrades decision quality. A team member who has been on for six hours without a real break is not operating at the same level they were at hour two, even if they feel like they are. The errors happen silently. The missed cue. The dropped detail. The delayed response. Rotate positions. Build breaks into every role assignment.

What to do Build a rotation schedule into the run-of-show. Every high-demand position has a backup who rotates in at scheduled intervals. Breaks are not optional — they are assignments.
🛡
Designate a Wellness Anchor — and Mean It

A wellness anchor is not a feel-good title. It is a function with a specific job: monitor the wellbeing of the team during conference week and act on what they see. Not as a secondary responsibility layered onto another role — as an actual designation with real authority. They watch for signs of burnout and fatigue, ensure people are eating and hydrating, prompt breaks, and flag anyone who is struggling before that struggle appears in a critical moment on the conference floor. This role should be named, briefed, and empowered before Boule begins.

What to do Name the wellness anchor before conference week. Give them explicit permission to pull people aside, redistribute tasks, and escalate wellbeing concerns the same way they would escalate a logistics problem. That permission needs to be given before the event begins.

"You cannot pour from an empty cup. And you cannot execute a seamless conference from a depleted team. Protection is not a soft concept. It is a project management requirement."
~ Dr. Jaye

Team Sustainability Principle
Pillar 3 of 3

When Something Goes Wrong —
Because Something Will

The plan will not execute perfectly. That is not pessimism. That is the honest reality of live events. The question is not whether something will go sideways. The question is whether the team has the composure, the clarity, and the systems to handle it without it cascading into something bigger.

🧭
The Problem First.
The Person After.

Under pressure, people conflate a missed task with a failed relationship. When something breaks on the conference floor, the team cannot afford to spend its cognitive energy on blame. The question is never "whose fault is this?" It is: what do we do right now? Save the accountability conversation for the debrief. During execution, the only thing that matters is solving the problem in front of you with the resources you have in that moment.

What to do Establish this norm before conference week, not during it. Say it out loud in a team meeting: during execution, we fix the problem first and debrief after. The team that agrees on this in advance does not waste time on it in the moment.
📋
Use a Live Issues Log — Every Time

When something goes wrong during the event, it needs to be captured immediately — not held in someone's head, not sent in a group chat that will be scrolled past, and not left to be reconstructed from memory during the post-conference debrief. A live issues log is a shared document, accessible to all leads, where any problem is logged in real time with what happened, who handled it, and what the resolution was. It becomes the institutional memory of execution day. The format is simple: timestamp, issue, who handled it, resolution. What makes the log powerful is consistency — every problem gets logged, not just the big ones.

What to do Set up the live issues log before conference week and train every lead on how to use it. Two minutes of documentation in the moment is worth two hours of reconstruction at the debrief. Every lead logs every issue. No exceptions for "it was minor." Minor issues logged consistently reveal patterns. Unlogged minor issues just repeat.
🤝
Let the Team Land Before the Next Thing Starts

The moment a conference ends, the instinct is to move — immediately. Debrief. Wrap-up emails. Next project. The team is still running on adrenaline, and that energy can feel like productivity. It is not. Decisions made in the 24 hours after a major event are often the least clear-headed ones in the entire cycle. The team needs a deliberate landing window before re-engaging with analysis or next steps. Not days — just enough time for the nervous system to discharge the intensity. Recovery is not delay. It is the condition that makes the debrief honest and the lessons useful rather than reactive.

What to do Build a 24–48 hour rest window into the post-conference calendar before any debrief or follow-up activity begins. Communicate this to the team before Boule opens so it is an expectation, not a surprise. The debrief — which has its own structure covered in the Meeting Facilitation section — produces better outcomes when the team arrives rested rather than still running.
Recharge

Tips & Tricks to Actually
Manage Stress

These are field-tested strategies for people doing real work under real pressure. Each one is distinct from what was covered in the Sanity section — these are personal practices, not team systems. Use what resonates. Share what helps.

Mindset
Name Your Pressure Points Before They Name You

Identify the three moments in the conference schedule that you personally dread the most. Write them down. Then ask: what specifically worries me about each one? Address the specifics now in planning. The fear of the unknown is almost always worse than the moment itself — and planning dissolves the unknown.

Mindset
Separate Your Identity from the Outcome

This team has invested a significant part of their personal identity into making Boule excellent. That investment is appropriate. It is also a source of disproportionate stress when something goes wrong, because a problem with the event can feel like a problem with the person. Practice this distinction before you need it: something went wrong does not mean you failed. It means something went wrong. The response is what defines you — not the problem.

Before It Starts
Finish Strong in the Final Two Weeks

The stress you feel during conference week is often stress that was created in the two weeks before it. Tasks left undone. Decisions left open. Confirmations left unsent. Push hard to close every open item before conference week begins. Arrival energy matters. Showing up with a list versus showing up ready is a completely different experience.

Before It Starts
Protect the 72 Hours Before the Event Opens

Decision quality is directly tied to rest. The planning team should not be pulling all-nighters in the final days. Final prep activities need to be scheduled and time-capped. Build a rule: after 10 p.m. in the 72 hours before the event opens, the only acceptable work is genuine emergencies. Everything else waits until morning.

During Execution
Eat. Hydrate. Move. On Purpose.

These sound basic. They are also the first things to disappear during a high-intensity event. Build your meals, your water, and a five-minute walk into your schedule the same way you build in meeting times. Not as an option. As a requirement. Your brain runs on glucose and hydration. When those drop, so does your judgment. Do not wait until you feel it — schedule it.

During Execution
Use the Five-Second Reset

When something goes sideways, take five seconds before you react. Not five minutes. Five seconds. Breathe in, slow exhale. That window is enough to shift from reactive to responsive — from a decision made in panic to a decision made with composure. The five-second reset is free, invisible to everyone around you, and more powerful than any other in-the-moment stress tool. Practice it before you need it.

During Execution
Say What You Need Out Loud

Teams under pressure often suffer in silence because everyone assumes everyone else is managing fine. If you need support, a swap, five minutes off the floor, or just someone to stand next to you for a minute — say it. Normalize asking. Normalize offering. It is how a high-performing team stays high-performing through the full duration of the event, not just the first half when energy is still high.

During Execution
Find One Thing to Celebrate in Real Time

Execution narrows your field of view to what is not working. Train yourself to also notice what is working — and name it out loud to the person responsible. A quick "that registration flow has been flawless" or "the way you handled that situation was excellent." Real-time recognition costs nothing and sustains team morale through the second half of the event, when reserves are lower and the work still demands full effort.

After It Is Over
Do Not Let the Adrenaline Disguise Exhaustion

The 24 hours after a major conference often feel energizing. The event is done. The pressure is released. The adrenaline is still circulating. That feeling is deceptive. What the body is actually doing is recovering from a sustained period of high-output stress. The decisions made in this window tend to be impulsive, reactive, or incomplete — even when they feel confident. Protect the recovery window. The debrief, the lessons learned, and the next steps will all be cleaner when they come from a rested team rather than one still running on fumes.

After It Is Over
Carry the Standard — Not Just the Lessons

Debriefs capture what to do differently next time. What they rarely capture is what to keep. Before you close the cycle, name — for yourself — the things you did well that you want to intentionally repeat. The way you stayed composed in that moment. The decision you made under pressure that turned out to be the right one. The way you supported a team member when the energy was low. Lessons learned are about the work. This is about the professional. Bring both to the next cycle.

The post-Boule meeting is not a celebration. It is not a complaint session. It is a structured, facilitated debrief — the professional close that a team of this caliber has earned and that the next planning cycle requires. The same facilitation disciplines that governed pre-Boule meetings apply here, with one addition: the goal is not to produce a plan. It is to produce truth.

01
Schedule It Before Boule Happens — and Protect That Date

The post-Boule debrief that gets scheduled after the conference almost never happens. Everyone is exhausted. The urgency is gone. The window closes. The debrief that was supposed to happen "when things settle down" becomes the debrief that never happened — and the lessons from this cycle get carried silently into the next one, where they repeat. Schedule the debrief date before the conference opens. Put it on the calendar as a protected commitment, not a tentative placeholder. Full team attendance is the expectation, not the hope.

Facilitator Practice Set the date during your final pre-Boule team meeting. Announce it as a standing commitment: "Our debrief is [date]. It is already on the calendar. We will all be there." That framing matters. It signals that the meeting is not optional and that the work does not end when the conference does.
02
Lead With Wins — Then Open the Floor for What Was Hard

The temptation in a post-event debrief is to go straight to what went wrong. Resist it. The team carried something significant, and that needs to be named before the critique begins. Start with specific wins — not general praise like "great job," but named, concrete moments: "The opening session ran flawlessly because of how the AV lead coordinated." Once wins are documented and heard, the team is in a more honest and less defensive place to discuss what was hard. That order is not courtesy — it is strategy.

Facilitator Practice Open the debrief with a structured round: each person names one thing that worked and one person on the team they want to specifically recognize. Record both. Then transition to challenges with the same structure: one thing that was harder than it should have been, and what you think caused it.
03
Use the Issues Log — Not Memory — as the Basis for Discussion

The quality of a post-Boule debrief depends almost entirely on the quality of the documentation captured during execution. A debrief built on memory is a debrief built on selective, emotional, and often incomplete recall. A debrief built on a live issues log — where problems were captured in real time with timestamps, owners, and resolutions — is a debrief built on facts. That distinction changes the conversation from "I feel like there were a lot of registration problems" to "we logged seven registration issues in the first two hours, and here is what they had in common." One produces a feeling. The other produces an action item.

Facilitator Practice Distribute the issues log to all leads 48 hours before the debrief. Ask each person to review their area and come prepared to discuss patterns — not just incidents. The facilitator's job in this session is to move the conversation from "what happened" to "what does this tell us about our planning and execution systems."
04
Distinguish Lessons Learned from Action Items

Most debriefs produce a list of lessons learned that no one ever acts on. The reason is simple: a lesson without an owner and a deadline is an observation, not a commitment. The post-Boule debrief needs to produce two distinct outputs. The first is a lessons-learned record — a documented account of what happened, what worked, and what did not, for institutional memory and future teams. The second is a short action item list — specific, named, time-bound changes to systems, processes, or role assignments that will be different next cycle. These are not the same document, and treating them as the same is why the same problems repeat.

Facilitator Practice At the end of the debrief, explicitly sort every item raised into one of two columns: "Lessons Learned" (captured for the record) or "Action Items" (assigned to an owner with a deadline). Nothing leaves the debrief ambiguous. The lessons-learned document goes into the planning archive. The action item list goes into the project tracker.
05
Acknowledge the Toll Before You Close the Meeting

The final agenda item of the post-Boule debrief is not a task item. It is a human one. Before the meeting closes, the facilitator creates space for the team to name what the work actually cost — not to dwell there, but to honor it. The late nights. The decision fatigue. The moments nobody outside this room witnessed. Teams that skip this step carry unacknowledged weight into the next cycle. Teams that name it together are lighter for it. That acknowledgment is not soft. It is what makes a team want to show up and do it again.

Facilitator Practice Close the debrief with one final round: "Name one thing this planning cycle asked of you personally — and one thing you are proud of." It does not need to be long. Three minutes. It completes the cycle with honesty and dignity. It is the professional close this team has earned.
